Modeling Oral Cholera Vaccine Request Behavior

Do reactive vaccine requests respond to short-term changes in cholera burden, or do they primarily reflect persistent differences between countries?

This page summarizes ongoing research in Lehigh University's Computational Simulation & Network Science Lab. Results are preliminary and may change as the research document is revised.

01 · Context

Reconstructing the path from outbreaks to vaccine requests

The global oral cholera vaccine stockpile supports preventive and reactive campaigns, but request, approval, and delivery records do not arrive as one analysis-ready dataset. The study links outbreak histories to vaccine requests across 25 countries from July 2013 through December 2019.

My work focused on building and validating the data pipeline, resolving inconsistent identifiers and timestamps, engineering lagged burden features, and producing datasets for statistical modeling and downstream reinforcement-learning research.

02 · Data construction

A balanced panel that keeps both action and inaction visible

The full panel contains 25 countries over 78 months. Countries that never submitted a request remain in the data: excluding them would condition the sample on the outcome itself and remove meaningful months in which a country had the opportunity to request vaccine but did not.

The 12-month burden specification uses a common estimation sample of 1,650 observations after allowing each country a full year of historical context.

Independent reconstruction

Rebuilt request-level records from original public sources because the comparison study's underlying dataset was unavailable.

External validation

Reconciled the reconstructed data against published summary statistics, matching 576 outbreaks and nearly every reported outbreak indicator.

Event normalization

Linked multiple delivery rounds to their requests and standardized request, decision, approval, and delivery dates.

Feature engineering

Created cumulative-burden windows and separated each country's long-run average from monthly deviation around that average.

03 · Study design

Separating persistent cross-country differences from short-term change

The primary outcome equals one when a country submitted at least one request classified as reactive in a given month. Logistic regression estimates two distinct associations: a between-country component for a country's average burden, and a within-country component for monthly deviation from its own average.

Repeated observations

Inference is clustered by country to account for repeated monthly observations within the same country.

Small-cluster inference

Wild cluster bootstrap p-values use 99,999 replications because conventional clustered inference can be unreliable with only 22–25 clusters.

Multiple time windows

Robustness checks repeat the analysis across 1-, 3-, 6-, and 12-month cumulative burden windows.

Sensitivity analysis

Models are re-estimated after progressively excluding the countries that received the most vaccine.

04 · Primary results

Persistent burden was more informative than short-term deviation

Positive association detected

95% CI [0.118, 0.265]; wild cluster bootstrap p = 0.00044.

Within countries β 0.042

No association detected

95% CI [-0.065, 0.148]; wild cluster bootstrap p = 0.46057.

Countries with higher long-run recorded cholera burden were more likely to submit reactive requests. The study did not detect a corresponding increase during months when a country's recent burden rose above its own usual level. A null result does not establish that short-term responsiveness is absent.

05 · Data reconciliation

Explaining a 35.1 million-dose discrepancy

The reconstructed records contain 89.09 million requested doses and 53.99 million approved doses. The difference can be decomposed into requests not approved, cancelled requests, records without a decision, and partial approvals.

06 · Interpretation

What this analysis can — and cannot — establish

The result points toward persistent differences between countries, potentially including surveillance, administrative capacity, institutional access, or reporting practices. Those mechanisms are plausible explanations, not measured causal effects.

  • The ecological country-month design does not link individual outbreaks to specific requests.
  • The analysis cannot estimate outbreak-to-request latency or judge whether a request was timely.
  • Twenty-five country clusters and 58 reactive request-months limit statistical precision.
  • Results from 2014–2019 should not be assumed to represent the post-2020 vaccine environment.
  • The associations should not be used as an automated vaccine-allocation rule.
